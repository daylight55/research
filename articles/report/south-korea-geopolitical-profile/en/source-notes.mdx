### Authoritative data / current context

- Korea.net anchors the constitutional structure, the strong presidency, and the unicameral legislature.
- The foreign ministry pages anchor the current policy frame for North Korea, the U.S. alliance, China, and Japan.
- Statistics Korea anchors the fertility, aging, and household-pressure discussion.
- The Bank of Korea anchors the rate and household-finance backdrop.
- MOTIE anchors exports, semiconductors, and shipbuilding.
- MCST anchors K-culture as an export and visibility asset.
- MMA anchors how military service affects education, work, and household formation.

### Secondary / interpretive context

- The report reads South Korean politics not only as conservative versus progressive competition, but also as the side effects of a strong presidency.
- It treats conglomerates, prosecutors, and the media as structural power centers rather than as isolated scandals.
- It treats Japan-South Korea relations as a practical security-and-trade connection, not only a history dispute.

## Evidence Notes for the Main Claims

- South Korea is best read as a country that deters North Korea through the U.S. alliance while balancing economic and security ties with China and Japan.
- As of autumn 2026, the president is Lee Jae Myung, and the government’s public frame still mixes dialogue and deterrence.
- The five-year single presidential term makes policy swings sharper at each transfer of power.
- China is large enough in trade terms that dependence on China and reliance on the United States are both structural constraints.
- Semiconductors and shipbuilding should be read as intersections of jobs, diplomacy, and supply chains, not just exports.
- Low fertility, housing pressure, and military service should be read together as one pressure bundle on younger households.
- The Japan-facing implication is that economic security, sea lanes, North Korea policy, procurement, and supply chains should be read together.

## Weakened or Excluded Material

- I did not foreground individual party scandals because they age quickly and can distort the long-run profile.
- I kept month-to-month headlines to a minimum because they make a durable country profile brittle.
- I kept the gender and conscription discussion at the institutional level to avoid needless sensationalism.

## Open Questions

- How much friction will stronger U.S.-ROK alignment create in South Korea’s China trade relationship?
- How far can low fertility and housing pressure shape younger voters over the long term?
- How resilient will practical cooperation with Japan be when historical disputes recur?
- Can shipbuilding, semiconductors, and batteries remain central to supply chains under geopolitical stress?
